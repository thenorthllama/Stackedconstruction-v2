## Introduction — Why we wrote this

Most people we sit down with have already talked to two or three contractors. They've collected wildly different numbers for what sounds like the same job, and they can't tell what the difference is. Some have a worse story: a project that stalled, a builder who stopped answering, work that had to be redone.

That confusion isn't your fault. Construction pricing is opaque by habit, and most contractors don't explain their process because they've never had to.

This guide is what we'd tell a friend before they hired anyone — including if that anyone isn't us. Read it, use it, and ask hard questions of everyone you talk to.

## Section 1 — Five decisions to make before you call anyone

You'll get better numbers, faster, if you can answer these five before your first meeting.

**1. How long are you staying?**
Renovating a home you'll be in for twenty years is a different project from one you'll sell in three. It changes the materials worth paying for and the ones that aren't.

**2. What's actually bothering you?**
"I hate my kitchen" is a starting point, not a brief. Is it the layout, the storage, the light, or the finishes? A good design conversation starts from the problem, not the product.

**3. What's your real range — and your ceiling?**
Not the number you want to say out loud. The number above which you'd walk away. A contractor who knows your ceiling can design to it. One who doesn't will design something you can't afford and you'll both waste a month.

**4. Are you willing to move out, and for how long?**
Living through a renovation is possible for most kitchen and bath work. Whole-home projects are harder. Deciding early affects both the schedule and the price.

**5. Who is the decision-maker?**
If two people need to agree on every selection, build that into the timeline. Selections are the single most common cause of delay we see — not trades, not materials.

## Section 2 — Design-build vs. hiring separately

There are two ways to get a renovation done. Neither is wrong. They cost you different things.

**Hiring separately:** you engage a designer or architect, get drawings, then take those drawings to contractors for bids.
- *Advantage:* competitive bids on identical plans, and a designer with no stake in construction cost.
- *What it costs you:* time, and the gap between the two. If the design comes back over budget, you're paying to redesign. And when something on site doesn't match the drawing, you're the one relaying messages between two companies who each think the other is wrong.

**Design-build:** one company designs and constructs.
- *Advantage:* the people drawing it know what it costs to build, so budget reality shows up during design instead of after. One contract, one point of contact, one company accountable when something doesn't line up.
- *What it costs you:* you're not bid-shopping the construction, so you have to trust the pricing. Which means you should ask for it line by line — see Section 4.

**How to decide:** if your project is large, architecturally complex, or you already have an architect you trust, hiring separately can be worth the coordination. If your priority is a realistic budget and one accountable party, design-build usually gets you there with less friction.

## Section 3 — What permitting actually takes here

Timelines vary with volume and season, and post-storm periods run longer. Confirm current turnaround with your contractor.

**Lee County (Fort Myers, Cape Coral, Bonita Springs)**
Interior remodels involving electrical, plumbing, or structural work require a permit. Plan for review time before work starts, plus inspections at rough-in and final.

**Collier County (Naples)**
Same requirement, and gated and coastal communities often add an HOA architectural review on top of county review — that's the step that surprises people. Start it early; it can run in parallel with county review.

**Two things worth knowing:**
- Only a licensed contractor can pull the permit. If someone suggests you pull it as the homeowner, that moves the liability to you. It's legal. It's rarely in your interest.
- Unpermitted work has to be disclosed when you sell, and buyers' inspectors find it. Fixing it later costs multiples of doing it right.

## Section 4 — Ten questions to ask any contractor

Ask all ten. The answers will separate people faster than any bid comparison.

**1. What's your license number, and may I verify it?**
Any real contractor answers instantly. Verify it yourself at the Florida DBPR license search — it takes under a minute and shows the license status, expiration, and any complaint history. *(Ours: CBC1269972.)*

**2. Are you insured, and can I see the certificate?**
General liability and workers' compensation. Ask for the certificate, not a yes.

**3. Will I get a line-item proposal or a lump sum?**
A line-item proposal shows what each part costs. A lump sum hides where the money goes and makes it impossible to compare bids or make trade-offs. Insist on line items.

**4. Who is my point of contact, and how often will I hear from them?**
Ask for a name and a frequency. "Call the office" is not an answer.

**5. Who is actually doing the work?**
Employees or subcontractors — either is fine. What matters is whether they've worked together before and who supervises them daily.

**6. What happens when something changes?**
Scope changes on almost every project. The question is whether changes get priced and approved in writing before work continues, or appear on the final invoice.

**7. What's your payment schedule?**
Payments should track completed work. A large deposit before anything happens is a warning sign. In Florida, be aware of your rights around lien releases as payments are made.

**8. What's your warranty?**
Get the period and what it covers, in writing.

**9. Can I see a project you finished more than two years ago?**
New work always looks good. Ask to see something that's been lived in.

**10. What could go wrong on my project?**
The most revealing question in the list. Anyone who says "nothing" either hasn't looked closely or isn't being straight with you. Experienced builders will name two or three specific risks in your house.

## Section 5 — Budgeting honestly

**Where the money actually goes in a kitchen.** Cabinetry is usually the largest single line, followed by countertops, then labor, then appliances, then everything else. That order matters: if you need to reduce cost, changing cabinet construction or countertop material moves the number far more than changing a faucet.

**Build in a contingency.** Ten to fifteen percent of project cost, set aside and untouched. Older homes hide things behind walls. A contingency turns a discovery into an inconvenience instead of a crisis.

**Three things that quietly add cost:**
- Moving plumbing or electrical (especially to a new island)
- Changing a wall that turns out to be load-bearing
- Selection changes after the order has been placed

**Three things that usually aren't worth it:**
- The highest tier of appliance if you cook occasionally
- Custom cabinetry where semi-custom would be invisible in the finished room
- Premium stone on surfaces nobody sees
